SURVEYING / FIELD NOTES · TECHNICAL NOTE 02

Why accurate field records
matter in surveying.

Reliable field notes help turn measurements into calculations, drawings and decisions that other people can review.

AUTHOR: YASHAVANTH ANTOPIC: SURVEYINGREADING: 4 MIN

Surveying is not only about taking measurements. It is also about recording exactly what was measured, how it was measured and under what conditions. Clear field records make the work easier to check, calculate and use later.

What should a field record contain?

The required information depends on the survey method and the assignment, but a useful record generally identifies the work and keeps observations understandable. Typical items include:

  • Project or site name, date and survey purpose.
  • Names or identifiers for the survey team and instrument.
  • Station names, point numbers and a clear sketch when required.
  • Observed readings with their units and sequence.
  • Instrument setup details, checks and relevant conditions.
  • Corrections, remarks, unusual observations and any repeated readings.

Keep units and labels consistent

Every measurement should have an unambiguous label and unit. Avoid mixing metres, millimetres, degrees or other units without clearly identifying the conversion. Point identifiers should also remain consistent between the field book, calculation sheet, drawing and digital file.

Check work before leaving the site

Where the method provides a check or closure calculation, carry it out while the team and equipment are still at the site. A missing observation or unclear station reference can be difficult to reconstruct later. Follow the checks and tolerances specified by the relevant assignment, project specification or applicable standard.

A good field book should allow another competent person to understand the survey sequence without having to guess what a note or number means.

Organise the digital record

When transferring observations to a spreadsheet or software package, preserve the original field record. Use descriptive file names, keep a copy of the raw observations and note any edits or calculations. Backups and version labels reduce the chance of losing the source information.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Writing readings without units or point identifiers.
  • Leaving corrections or repeated observations unexplained.
  • Mixing raw observations with calculated values without labels.
  • Transcribing numbers without checking them against the field record.
  • Sharing a final drawing without recording its source data and revision.

Closing thought

Accurate surveying depends on a complete chain from observation to final output. Careful field notes are a simple but essential part of that chain, helping the survey remain transparent, repeatable and easier to verify.
